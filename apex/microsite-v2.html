Chapter 01

What is a compression fracture?

Your spine is a stack of small bones called vertebrae. A compression fracture happens when one of those bones weakens and collapses — usually in the mid or lower back. Instead of a clean break like a fractured arm, the vertebra compresses and loses height, like a can crushed from above.

Vertebral compression fracture (VUR-tuh-brul · kum-PRESH-un)

A collapse of one of the bones of the spine, most often caused by bone thinning (osteoporosis). Even everyday movements — a cough, a sneeze, lifting groceries — can be enough to cause one when bone is fragile.

Most compression fractures are the result of osteoporosis, which is why they're most common in adults over 50 — and why many people don't connect the sudden pain to a broken bone. Left untreated, multiple fractures can lead to height loss, a stooped posture, and chronic pain.

Chapter 02

How to recognize one.

Compression fractures are easy to dismiss because the symptoms overlap with ordinary back pain. These are the signs worth paying attention to:

  1. Sudden back pain with no obvious injury

    Pain that appears abruptly — sometimes after something as minor as a sneeze, a bump in the car, or picking something up.

  2. Pain that worsens when standing or walking

    And often eases when lying down. Ordinary muscle strains rarely follow this pattern so consistently.

  3. Losing height

    Each compressed vertebra can take centimeters off your height. If you've "shrunk" over the years, fractures may be the reason.

  4. A stooped or hunched posture

    Multiple fractures in the front of the vertebrae tip the spine forward — the rounded upper back sometimes called a dowager's hump.

When it's urgent

Back pain with numbness, tingling, weakness in the legs, or loss of bladder or bowel control can signal nerve compression and needs attention right away. Call (208) 800-9040 or seek emergency care.

Chapter 03

Who's at risk, and why.

Healthy vertebrae don't collapse under everyday load. Compression fractures almost always point to weakened bone — and these are the most common reasons bone weakens:

Osteoporosis

By far the leading cause. Bone loses density with age — especially in women after menopause.

Age over 50

Risk climbs steadily with age for both women and men.

Long-term steroid use

Medications like prednisone accelerate bone loss when used for months or years.

A previous fracture

One compression fracture makes the next one several times more likely — treating the first matters.

Smoking & heavy alcohol use

Both interfere with the body's ability to rebuild bone.

Certain cancers

Less commonly, tumors in the spine weaken vertebrae — one reason unexplained back pain deserves imaging.

Chapter 04

How we treat it.

Treatment starts with an exam and imaging to confirm the fracture and rule out other causes. From there, care usually follows one of two paths — and at Apex, we always start with the least invasive option that fits your situation.

First Path

Conservative care

Many fractures heal on their own in 8–12 weeks with the right support while the bone mends.

  • Short-term pain medication
  • Bracing to support the spine
  • Modified activity — not bed rest
  • Bone-density treatment to prevent the next fracture
When Pain Persists

Kyphoplasty

A minimally invasive procedure that stabilizes the collapsed vertebra from the inside — often relieving pain within days.

  • A small balloon gently restores vertebral height
  • Medical-grade bone cement stabilizes the fracture
  • Performed through a needle — no open incision
  • Light sedation, outpatient setting

Chapter 05

Common questions.

How do I know if my back pain is a fracture?

You can't tell from symptoms alone — that's the problem. If your pain came on suddenly, worsens with standing, and you're over 50 or have osteoporosis, it's worth an exam. A standard X-ray or MRI confirms the diagnosis in minutes.

Does a compression fracture heal on its own?

Often, yes — typically within 8–12 weeks with conservative care. But "healed" isn't always "pain-free": a vertebra that heals in a collapsed position can cause lasting posture changes and pain, which is why persistent cases deserve a specialist's look.

Is kyphoplasty safe?

Kyphoplasty has been performed for over two decades and is considered a low-risk, minimally invasive procedure. As with any procedure there are risks, which your Apex provider will review with you — and we'll only recommend it when the likely benefit clearly outweighs them.

Will my insurance cover treatment?

Diagnosis and treatment of compression fractures — including kyphoplasty when medically indicated — are covered by Medicare and most major insurance plans. Our team verifies your coverage before any procedure, so there are no surprises.

What happens if I just ignore it?

Some fractures heal without issue. But untreated fractures can heal collapsed, and each fracture significantly raises the risk of another — a cycle that leads to height loss, stooped posture, and chronic pain. At minimum, get the first one diagnosed.
